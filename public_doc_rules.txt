CORPORATOR DOCUMENTATION — STRUCTURE AND WRITING BRIEF

PROJECT GOAL

Create the English-language documentation for Corporator, a browser-based 3D city-management game.

The documentation should feel like a useful game guide written by someone who understands the game. It needs enough personality to be enjoyable, enough detail to help players make decisions, and enough technical precision to explain how funds, contracts, and verification actually work.

Do not turn it into a marketing pitch, a corporate whitepaper, or a repository audit.

This document defines the structure and editorial approach. It is not the finished set of player-facing articles.

Website layout and visual design are outside the scope of this stage.


1. WHAT THE DOCUMENTATION SHOULD DO

Help readers answer three sets of questions:

UNDERSTAND THE GAME
What do I control?
What do I actually do?
What makes one decision better than another?

LEARN TO RUN A CITY
How do buildings, workers, resources, and schedules fit together?
Why is something idle?
What should I develop next?

UNDERSTAND THE ECONOMY
Where does Capital come from?
Who pays for work?
Where are my tokens held?
What can I verify?
What can prevent a withdrawal?

The main reading path should move from the city to its workforce, development, economy, and underlying protocol.

Readers who want to inspect risks or contracts first must be able to do so directly.


2. CORE FACTS TO PRESERVE

Corporator is about operating an existing city, not building a procedural map from scratch.

The physical island is authored and shared. Each account has its own city economy. A shared map does not mean shared control of workers, buildings, or balances.

Players activate and operate building functions at authored locations. The current grid-based build command is an implementation mismatch, not a second intended placement system.

$CORP is an external token.

Capital is an internal accounting unit backed by $CORP in the Vault. It is not an ERC-20 and is not the same as a wallet’s $CORP balance.

Payroll is finite. A valid production request does not guarantee a full payout.

Backing in $CORP does not guarantee a stable dollar value, immediate liquidity, or profit.

Stock Tokens are external assets used for industry access. Their exact legal and economic rights come from the external asset, not from Corporator.

Using company names and tickers does not establish an official partnership.

Agents are deterministic workers. An LLM does not calculate their economic output.

There are five current worker roles:
Engineer, Logistician, Trader, Scientist, and Manager.

Operator is not a sixth role in the current content.

There are six industries:
Compute / NVDA
Energy / TSLA
Logistics / AMZN
Consumer / AAPL
Automation / MSFT
Finance / COIN

Energy and Compute are consumable resources. Logistics is not a third inventory resource.

There are 18 building types:
six base buildings, six industry buildings, and six synergy buildings.

A city day lasts 25 real hours and contains 24 economic ticks.
One tick lasts 62 minutes and 30 seconds in real time.

The base offline cap is 12 city hours. Authored Automation progression can extend it to 18 city hours.


Bank operates from 08:00 to 20:00 in personal city time and is closed on city Sunday.

The full intended player loop is not yet implemented end to end.

Do not treat a content entry, formula, screen, button, contract primitive, or mock result as proof of a working player-facing feature.

4. GETTING STARTED

WELCOME TO CORPORATOR

Start with the central idea:

The city is already built. Your job is to make it work.

Introduce the browser game, the authored island, and the player’s role as its operator.

Describe the decisions players make:
activating functions, hiring workers, assigning tasks, supplying resources, opening industries, and choosing what to improve.

Make the difference between the shared physical map and personal economic state easy to understand.

Do not begin with contract architecture or the Vault formula.

Introduce financial limits briefly and plainly. This is an economic game, not a promise of guaranteed returns.


HOW THE GAME WORKS

Walk through the main loop:

Obtain Capital
→ activate a function
→ assign a worker where required
→ meet the working conditions
→ complete a shift
→ receive the applicable payout
→ choose the next investment.

Explain what the player decides at each step.

Distinguish production from payment early. A valid request can still be trimmed by Payroll.

Include reinvestment, industry access, and Bank withdrawal as parts of the broader loop without suggesting that all of them work in the current build.


YOUR FIRST SESSION

Separate demo entry from wallet-connected entry.

Explain:
- What a login signature is for.
- How signing in differs from approving a transaction.
- Why demo and wallet-backed progress cannot cross.
- What the current version actually allows a new player to do.

Do not publish a deposit walkthrough before the application supports deposits.


WHAT’S AVAILABLE NOW

Provide a short, factual status page with three categories:
- Available.
- Partially implemented.
- In development.

This is not an internal bug tracker.

It must still distinguish a working player feature from a local contract test, a generated rule, or a placeholder screen.

Date status claims and identify the build or rules version they describe.


5. RUNNING YOUR CITY

THE ISLAND AND ITS BUILDINGS

Describe the city as existing infrastructure with functions the player can activate and operate.

Clarify:
- The island is authored, not generated from a wallet.
- City economies are personal.
- Physical buildings and active economic functions are different things.
- An inactive building can remain visible on the island.

Mention the legacy grid implementation only in a status note. Do not teach it as an intended alternative.


CITY TIME

Cover:
- 25 real hours per city day.
- 24 ticks per city day.
- 62 minutes and 30 seconds per tick.
- The personal city calendar.
- Building schedules.
- Bank and Exchange hours.
- City Sunday versus external market weekends.

Give practical examples of what these rules mean.

A player should leave knowing why a building is closed, not just how to convert time units.


PRODUCTION AND RESOURCES

Answer the question:
What has to be true for work to happen?

Follow the chain:
function → assignment → task → schedule → resources → result → payment.

Explain Energy and Compute separately.

Describe Logistics as idle reduction, not warehouse stock.

Separate:
- Direct Capital-producing functions.
- Resource-producing functions.
- Support and service functions.

Explain why a support building can be valuable without directly producing Capital.


CITY METRICS

For each metric, state:
- What it measures.
- How it is derived.
- Whether it directly changes production.
- What a player can learn from it.

Preserve these distinctions:
- GDP is not net profit.
- Productivity is a measurement, not a second output bonus.
- Happiness does not currently apply an active output penalty.
- Stability measures valid working ticks under the applicable rules.
- Influence is a seasonal score, not spendable currency.

Do not imply that the current interface already receives every metric.


WHILE YOU’RE AWAY

Explain:
- The base offline window.
- The role of building schedules during eligible offline time.
- Automation’s authored offline-cap progression.
- What happens after the cap is reached.

Separate intended catch-up behavior from the incomplete server integration.


6. YOUR WORKFORCE

UNDERSTANDING AGENTS

Introduce the worker model:

Role + level + traits + assignment + task.

The player makes management decisions. Workers execute the assigned work under deterministic rules.

Do not imply that agents independently spend funds, govern the city, or use an LLM to determine payouts.


ROLES AND ASSIGNMENTS

Engineer:
Production and energy infrastructure.

Logistician:
Logistics and idle reduction.

Trader:
Finance, exchange, and retail functions.

Scientist:
Research and computing infrastructure.

Manager:
Administration and city focus.

Explain home, neighboring, permitted foreign, and incompatible assignments.

Use actual building examples from the content.

Do not add Operator without a new, explicit content decision.


TASKS AND TRAITS

Present choices as trade-offs, not a list of free bonuses.

Examples:
- Overclock increases output and Energy cost.
- Stockpile offers stronger idle reduction with an output penalty.
- Frugal reduces resource costs at the expense of some output.
- Specialist performs better at home and worse away.

Put exact values beside the explanation when useful.

A modifier percentage is not a promise of the same percentage increase in payouts or profit.


LEVELS AND CAPACITY

Explain worker development, available slots, city-level limits, and the infrastructure that affects them.

Do not invent firing rules, worker markets, or agent NFT ownership.

STAKING AND INDUSTRY ACCESS

Explain:
- External Stock Tokens are used.
- Access thresholds are measured in token quantity.
- The authored minimum thresholds are frozen rather than repriced in dollars every day.
- Initial access, extra stake bonuses, and progression requirements are separate concepts.
- Unstake has intended grace-period and freeze rules.
- Those rules are not yet fully enforced in the gameplay implementation.

Do not describe staking as guaranteed yield or imply that the game mints its own NVDA.


MARKETS AND EFFICIENCY

Begin with:

Markets change the conditions an industry works under. They do not credit stock-price gains to your city balance.

Then explain:

- The moving-average comparison.
- Recorded external inputs.
- Stale-data handling.
- Which modifiers must be applied in the actual simulation.

Keep stock performance, district efficiency, city payouts, and player profit distinct.

13. WRITING STYLE

Write in natural English for players.

Use concrete subjects and verbs. Explain what happens, why it happens, and what the player can do.

Use contractions where they sound natural.

Vary sentence length. Keep paragraphs readable without making every sentence a standalone slogan.

Avoid empty phrases such as:
“unlock limitless potential”
“revolutionary ecosystem”
“seamless experience”
“next-generation AI economy”
“embark on your journey”
“maximize your earning potential”

Do not repeatedly use “not just X, but Y” or end every section with a motivational line.

A small amount of corporate humor is appropriate in gameplay explanations.

Example:
“Another Workshop won’t solve a power shortage. It will give you two Workshops waiting for power.”

Do not use jokes in explanations of custody, fees, withdrawals, losses, or emergency controls.

Prefer:

“Your Workshop is active, but it still needs a compatible worker and enough Energy to run.”

Avoid:

“Production functionality is governed by a multi-layer operational dependency framework.”

Do not overuse fictional office memos, executive briefings, or corporate roleplay. The game’s identity should support the explanation, not obstruct it.

Do not invent lore, ownership rights, mechanics, or missing details.

If something needs a new design decision, identify it as a proposal before presenting it as part of the game.


14. RULES VERSUS IMPLEMENTATION

Keep approved design and working functionality separate.

A useful status note is:

Rules: Defined in the current content pack.
Implementation: Partial.

Money-related pages must also state whether the player-facing operation is available.

Do not interrupt every paragraph with a warning. Put concise status notes where they are useful, and make critical availability limits visible before instructions that would otherwise suggest the player can act.

Preserve unresolved contradictions. Do not silently choose whichever version makes the game sound more complete.

The supplied handoff is the basis for these claims. It is not a substitute for verifying a newer repository state or production deployment.


15. STANDARD ARTICLE FORMAT

Use this structure where it helps:

1. WHAT IT IS
A short introduction that answers the player’s immediate question.

2. HOW IT WORKS
A clear cause-and-effect explanation.

3. YOUR DECISION
What the player controls and what the trade-offs are.

4. EXAMPLE
A small, clearly labeled scenario.

5. REQUIREMENTS AND LIMITS
Prerequisites, exceptions, and failure conditions.

6. EXACT RULES
Formulas or tables when they add useful precision.

7. CURRENT STATUS
What works in the relevant build.

8. RELATED READING
The next useful pages.

Do not force every article into all eight sections.
Avoid repeating the same disclaimer, summary, and transition on every page.


16. SAMPLE OPENING

THE CITY IS ALREADY BUILT. NOW MAKE IT WORK.

In Corporator, you don’t start with an empty map. You take charge of an island with workshops, energy infrastructure, research facilities, and a bank. The buildings are there. Getting them to work together is your job.

A Workshop needs more than an active switch. It needs a suitable worker and enough Energy. More computing capacity puts pressure on the power supply. A faster task can raise consumption. And even a properly configured shift depends on the budget available to pay it.

You decide which problem to solve first.

Real Stock Tokens unlock six industries, while external markets influence their working conditions. A rising stock price doesn’t automatically become your profit, though. The city still runs on resources, schedules, and a finite Payroll fund.

This guide explains those connections, from your first assignment to the contracts behind the economy.
